How to Keep Your Brand Consistent Across Every Location: A Multi-Location Print Program Guide

Same brand, every location, every reorder.

November 24, 2026

It usually starts small. One location runs out of table tents and prints a few at a nearby shop. Another updates a flyer with its own hours and "improves" the layout while it's at it. A third reorders last season's poster from an old file.

Six months later, you walk into three locations and see three versions of your brand. The reds don't match. The paper feels different. One window cling has the old logo.

Nobody did anything malicious. There was just no system. A multi-location print program is that system: a set of templates, specs, and rules that keep every location on brand while still letting local teams do their jobs.

This guide is for operations leaders at restaurant, retail, hospitality, and healthcare chains who want to stop print drift before it spreads.

What is a multi-location print program?

A multi-location print program is a standardized way of producing and reordering printed materials across many sites. It defines:

  • What gets printed (your recurring kit)
  • How it looks (approved templates and specs)
  • Who can change what (permissions and approvals)
  • How it gets ordered and updated (workflow and version control)

The goal isn't to take control away from local teams. It's to make the right thing the easy thing, so a manager at any location can reorder materials in minutes without anyone going off brand.

Why print drifts across locations

Brand drift is almost always an operations problem, not a design problem. The most common causes:

  • No single source of truth. Several versions of the same file live in inboxes, shared drives, and desktops.
  • Local workarounds. When the official process is slow or unclear, locations find their own printer.
  • Vague specs. "Glossy flyer" can mean ten different paper stocks and finishes.
  • Different vendors. Each printer interprets color, stock, and finish a little differently.
  • Unclear ownership. Nobody knows who is allowed to change what, so everyone changes whatever they need to.
  • Last-minute needs. A promotion starts tomorrow, and a rush job skips every check.

Each of these is fixable, and the fixes are the four building blocks below.

What's in a recurring multi-location kit?

Most chains reorder the same group of items over and over. These are the best candidates for a program because the volume is steady and the risk of drift is highest.

Item Where it lives What tends to drift
Flyers Counters, doors, local mailings Layouts, offers, and contact details
Brochures Lobbies, waiting areas, front desks Fold, paper stock, outdated information
Table tents Dining tables, lobby tables Promotions, sizing, finish
Counter mats Checkout and service counters Color, scale, and wear over time
Window clings Storefront windows and doors Logo versions, sizing, placement
Posters Walls, lobbies, in-store displays Resolution, color, outdated campaigns
Banners Entrances, patios, event spaces Sizing, finishes, messaging

These seven categories cover most of what a location needs day to day. Standardize them first.

Building block 1: Locked templates

A locked template is an approved layout where the brand elements can't be changed, and only specific fields are open for local edits.

Locked (HQ controls):

  • Logo and logo placement
  • Brand colors
  • Fonts and type sizes
  • Layout and grid
  • Legal, compliance, and disclaimer text

Editable (local teams control):

  • Location name, address, and phone number
  • Hours of operation
  • Local contact or manager name
  • Approved local promotions, within set limits

Build one master template per item, with clear instructions about which fields can be edited. Where possible, use a format that prevents accidental changes, such as a template with fixed layers or an internal form-based ordering process.

Building block 2: Approved specs

If your templates define how a piece looks, your specs define how it's made. Without specs, the same file can print very differently depending on the paper, finish, and printer.

Create a print spec sheet for each item in your kit. For each one, define:

  • Size and fold: Exact finished dimensions, plus folds for brochures (see our guide to standard brochure sizes and folds)
  • Stock: Paper type and weight, or material for signs and clings
  • Finish: Gloss, matte, uncoated, or laminated
  • Color: CMYK values for every brand color, and Pantone references as a color target where needed; confirm achievable matching and proofing with your printer (see RGB vs CMYK)
  • Bleed and margins: Consistent file setup, as in our prepress checklist
  • Quantity guidelines: Typical order sizes per location

Building block 3: Version control

Version control sounds technical, but the idea is simple: everyone should always be able to tell which file is current.

Four practices cover most of it:

  1. One home for files. Keep all approved templates and print-ready files in a single, controlled place. Not in email, not on individual desktops.
  2. Clear file names. Include the item, campaign or season, and version, such as TableTent_Winter2026_v3.
  3. Retire old files. When a version is replaced, archive it and remove it from the active folder so it can't be reordered by accident.
  4. Keep a change log. Record what changed, who approved it, and when. It makes audits and troubleshooting simple.

For anything with prices, allergen information, legal wording, or medical disclaimers, version control is also a compliance issue. A reprint from an outdated file can put incorrect information in front of customers or patients.

Building block 4: Who may change what

This is the part most programs skip, and it's where most drift begins. Spell out exactly who can change what, in writing.

A simple three-tier model works well:

Tier Who decides What it covers Examples
Tier 1: Free local edits Location manager Pre-defined fields in the template Address, phone, hours, local manager name
Tier 2: Approval needed Regional or marketing lead Content that goes beyond the fields Local promotions, event messaging, new images
Tier 3: HQ only Brand or marketing team Anything that defines the brand Logo, colors, fonts, layouts, legal text, new items

A few notes on making it work:

  • Make approvals fast. If approval takes a week, people will go around it. Set a target response time.
  • Make the tiers visible. Put the permissions chart wherever people order.
  • Name an owner. Someone should be responsible for the program, even if it's a shared role.
  • Plan for exceptions. Occasionally a location needs something special. A simple exception request is better than a workaround.

How to set up your program, step by step

Step 1: Audit what's out there. Collect examples of every printed item from several locations. Lay them side by side. You'll see exactly where the drift is.

Step 2: Choose your core kit. Start with the recurring items listed above. Don't try to standardize everything at once.

Step 3: Build master templates. Create a locked template for each item, with editable fields clearly marked.

Step 4: Write the spec sheet. Document size, stock, finish, and color for each item.

Step 5: Set permissions and approvals. Define the three tiers, assign owners, and set response-time targets.

Step 6: Set up ordering. Give locations a single, simple way to reorder with approved templates and specs. Keep approved files and specifications in your own controlled system, and share the current versions with your print partner when requesting a quote.

Step 7: Roll out and train. Introduce the program with a one-page guide. Show managers how to order and what they can change.

Step 8: Review regularly. Check a few locations each quarter. Compare what's on the walls to your standards, and update templates and specs as your brand evolves.

Industry notes

The program is the same, but each industry has different details to watch.

  • Restaurants: Menus, table tents, and counter mats change often. Keep pricing and required allergen language under designated approval, and let locations edit only what's truly local.
  • Retail: Seasonal posters, window clings, and in-store banners need to roll out at the same time. Use version names tied to campaigns and dates.
  • Hospitality: Guest-facing materials set the tone of the stay. Keep paper stock, finish, and color tightly specified so every property feels the same.
  • Healthcare: Patient-facing brochures, posters, and signage often carry required disclaimers or compliance language supplied by your organization. Lock that text, and route any changes through an approval step.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Trying to control everything. If the process is too rigid, locations will work around it. Allow local fields where they genuinely help.
  • Leaving specs vague. "Glossy" is not a spec. Write down stock, weight, and finish.
  • Skipping the owner. A program with no clear owner slowly falls apart.
  • Never retiring old files. If it's still in the folder, someone will print it.
  • Standardizing the design but not the production. A perfect template printed at three different shops will still look like three different brands.
  • Forgetting to update. Brand standards change. Build a regular review into your calendar.

What this means for your business

Consistency doesn't happen by accident. It's built, one decision at a time, into how your materials are designed, produced, and reordered.

  • Make the right thing easy. The best program is the one locations actually use.
  • Write it down. Specs, permissions, and file rules only work if they exist on paper.
  • Standardize production, not just design. Your brand lives in the paper, the finish, and the color as much as the layout.
  • Start with the recurring kit. A handful of items covers most of your volume and most of your risk.

Build a print program that scales

A multi-location print program takes some setup, but once it's in place, every location can reorder with confidence and every guest, patient, or customer sees the same brand.
